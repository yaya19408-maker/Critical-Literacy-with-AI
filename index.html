Evaluating Artificial Intelligence Feedback
in an AI-Enhanced Critical Literacy Writing Class

Advisor: Yi-Hsuan Gloria Lo
Department of Applied Foreign Languages, National Taiwan University of Science and Technology, Taipei, 106, Taiwan, ROC
Advisee: Chih-Wei Clement Chang
Department of Applied Foreign Languages, National Taiwan University of Science and Technology, Taipei, 106, Taiwan, ROC
Research Highlights
  • AI feedback addresses formal grammar elements but shows limitations in text coherence.
  • Students exhibit high acceptance of grammatical form changes and high rejection of content revisions.
  • The PTCF framework optimizes generative AI feedback quality and contextual accuracy.
  • Critical composition tasks simultaneously foster domain literacy and functional AI literacy.
繁體中文

Abstract

Given the pervasiveness of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and the ensuing pedagogical shift of critical literacy education, it is substantial to explore how students can cultivate AI literacy in the critical literacy classroom. Existing literature accentuates AI feedback for literacy development and the enablement for students to critically evaluate both capabilities and limitations of AI. However, empirical studies combining AI and critical literacy for real-world actions remain scarce. Thus, this study investigates how students accept, modify, or reject AI feedback and evaluate its contextual alignment and usefulness to examine their understanding concerning capabilities and limitations of AI. This study focuses on four students in two groups. In the course, students write the social action project following critical literacy framework by Lewison et al. (2002) and structured prompt engineering to collaborate with AI on their essays. This study collects students' writing drafts and final projects, chat history with AI, reflection journals, and semi-structured post-interviews to examine how they process AI feedback and the perceptions of capabilities and limitations. The materials will be analyzed through the lens of AI literacy framework by Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) to understand how students evaluate prompt effectiveness and AI feedback to proceed with their social actions. By observing the decisions on AI feedback, this study aims to examine students' thoughts in the process of engaging, creating with, managing, and designing AI for further implications of AI in future critical literacy classrooms.

Keywords: Artificial Intelligence (AI), AI literacy, EFL writing, critical literacy, feedback, prompt engineering

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) feedback presents distinct capabilities within modern writing instruction, functioning as an immediate, quick, personalized, tailored, adaptive, flexible, scalable, and expandable educational intervention. In standard composition contexts, AI feedback effectively addresses surface-level linguistic features, categorized as formal elements including grammar and word choice. However, automated evaluations often exhibit acute limitations regarding deep structural content, such as text cohesion and coherence. Empirical literature reveals a systemic dichotomy in student engagement with automated revision tools: individuals demonstrate high acceptance rates for feedback concerning grammatical form, juxtaposed with high rejection rates regarding content changes. This evaluative gap manifests prominently across argumentative and general academic writing tasks. Rejections of content-based feedback typically stem from contextual misalignment—where suggestions conflict with student intention or pedagogical expectations—or outright irrelevance to the specific writing topic. Crucially, existing studies have failed to investigate the precise evaluative criteria students employ when deciding to accept, modify, or reject AI suggestions (Gap 1).

Uncritical alignment with large language models introduces substantial educational bottlenecks. Recent neurocognitive investigations by the MIT Media Lab (2025) demonstrate that overreliance on AI tools during composition tasks leads to an accumulation of "cognitive debt" and negatively impacts executive brain functions. While automated scaffolding can amplify advanced academic synthesis when moderated by robust independent reasoning, unreflective uptake of every suggestion diminishes original student voice. Pozdniakov (2026) asserts that treating automated "suggestions" as absolute "corrections" indicates a structural deficit in internal evaluative judgment and basic AI literacy.

To institutionalize balanced interactions, the OECD (2025) AI Literacy Framework establishes a tiered engagement process incorporating input prompting (inquiry), experiential processing of system capabilities and systemic risk limits, and iterative output evaluation. Competent AI literacy requires students to actively engage, create, manage, and design alongside automated tools, utilizing explicit evaluative operations categorized into accepting, modifying, or rejecting generated directives. When equipped with these evaluative mechanisms, student writers can successfully integrate AI feedback within content-focused, theme-based writing spaces like critical literacy. Traditional literacy denotes the capacity to read, write, and express meaning. Critical literacy operationalizes text to disrupt commonplaces, interrogate multiple viewpoints, view issues through sociopolitical lenses, challenge systemic power hierarchies, and implement real-world praxis. Because critical literacy depends entirely on localized social, cultural, and political dynamics, it remains strictly content-focused. As highlighted by the OECD (2025), artificial intelligence lacks an authentic grasp of real-world context, leaving human agents uniquely responsible for contextual judgments. Consequently, an empirical void persists regarding how student decision-making under AI feedback conditions shapes content-focused, socio-politically situated writing and subsequent real-world praxis (Gap 2).

Research Questions

The present study addresses this intersection through two formal research formulations:

RQ1: In what ways do students accept, modify, or reject the feedback generated by AI in their critical literacy writing projects?
RQ2: What are the perceptions of students concerning the prompt design, contextual alignment of AI, and its usefulness in critical literacy writing?

Methodology

Research Setting and Curricular Framework

This study investigated a 16-week, compulsory undergraduate course titled "Critical Literacy: Theme-based English Reading and Writing (I)" hosted at a university of science and technology in Northern Taiwan. The primary research cohort comprised 33 first-year Applied Foreign Languages (EFL) students exhibiting a verified average proficiency level of CEFR B2. Participants possessed basic functional computer literacy but lacked exposure to critical literacy frameworks before enrollment. The software deployment featured OpenAI’s ChatGPT GPT-5 mini platform. The instructional design implemented the Four Dimensions of Critical Literacy framework advanced by Lewison, Flint, and van Sluys (2002):

1. Disrupting the Commonplace: Students learn to deconstruct everyday objects, beliefs, or policies previously accepted as neutral or static ("it is what it is"). Operations: Reading foundational academic literature and leveraging AI models for collaborative topic brainstorming.
2. Interrogating Multiple Viewpoints: Identifying marginalized viewpoints, omissions, and multiple points of view to establish multi-layered texts. Operations: Gathering five external references and using structured queries to challenge single narratives via AI.
3. Focusing on Sociopolitical Issues: Investigating dynamics of systemic power, societal structures, cultural hegemonies, and politics underlying the chosen topic. Operations: Deep source evaluation and collaborative logical refinement with AI assistants.
4. Taking Social Action to Promote Social Justice: Designing realistic, contextually viable localized interventions to mitigate identified inequities. Operations: Developing practical action designs and utilizing AI engines to pressure-test execution steps.

Sampling, Data Collection, and Analytical Models

Using purposive sampling based on distinct thinking styles and interactive modes, researchers narrowed a 10-student cohort across two primary target teams down to four individual focal case studies: Jeffrey (the technical group strategist) and Alexander (the critical vision group leader) from the "Social Separation" project team; and Roy (reflective learner) and Joanne (context-driven pragmatist) from the "Period Poverty" project team.

Data streams included continuous composition reflection journals, structural tracking of Draft 4 and 5 modifications, complete conversation logs, and final open-ended evaluation summaries documenting system pros and cons.

Preliminary Results & Discussion (Case: Jeffrey)

Process Tracing (RQ1)

PTCF Prompt Construction Case

During the initial 'Disrupting the Commonplace' phase, Jeffrey’s team constructed the following standardized prompt matrix:

"Persona: Act as a group advisor who can discuss and give us answers for our questions and is good at finding key points.
Tasks: Help me identify 3 commonplaces that locals, overseas, and international students might hold. They could be beliefs that are often presented as neutral or 'it is what it is' or things or actions people do or take without critically understanding what they are and why they are.
Context: We are a first year student in NTUST Taiwan majoring in Applied Foreign Language. We have a critical literacy course which requires us to apply the first dimension of critical literacy which is 'disrupting the commonplace'. The topic we have decided on is how in our campus it is filled with locals, overseas, and also international students in which they prefer to socialize with their own 'kind' of people.
Examples: For instance, a 'commonplace' might be how locals might avoid communicating with international students due to differences they might have (to not offend them in some way).
Format: Please present your answer in bullet points and elaborate on each points with easy to understand sentences, if there's an example, you can also provide that.
Tone: Use an academic, critical, and open-minded tone".

Linguistic Clarity and Sociopolitical Conceptual Acceptance

Tracing text progression verified that Jeffrey systematically accepted AI interventions focused on local sentence-level clarity and global sociopolitical framing. In Draft 4’s introduction, the team’s original viewpoint read: "From my point of view, the divide can lead to students' anxiety and pressure." The system flagged this phrasing as structurally abrupt and vague, noting a failure to specify which sub-cohort experienced isolation anxieties. The model generated a clearer alternative: "In my view, this divide heightens anxiety and pressure particularly for international students, who must navigate unfamiliar social norms." Jeffrey fully integrated this text revision.

Similarly, regarding the 'Interrogating Multiple Viewpoints' dimension, the system advised expanding Body Paragraph 1 to address counterpoints, specifically detailing how local student cohorts might reinforce social divides via unconscious privilege. Jeffrey accepted this guidance to add depth to his analysis. Furthermore, during sociopolitical evaluation, the platform recommended framing well-intentioned administrative orientation events as potential ideological blind spots that isolate international groups from day one. Jeffrey incorporated this critical feedback, revising the text to state that special separate support activities "can accidentally make things unfair" and cause marginalized students to be "always seen as different or separate". This phase illustrates how AI can introduce complex academic terminology to support sociopolitical analysis.

Strategic Modification and Contextual Localization

Feedback modification occurred when automated ideas were practical but required localization. For their final social action plan, Jeffrey's group requested options for organizing an open student forum. The language model recommended incorporating financial incentives and competitive lures to boost attendance. While recognizing its utility, the group modified this suggestion because commercialized competitions conflicted with their intended open dialogue format. They replaced cash rewards with free group meals, blending automated suggestions with their own ideas to fit their constraints.

Rationale-Based Rejection Dynamics

Jeffrey systematically rejected suggestions characterized by excessive generality or context mismatch. When the system recommended deleting older core literature citations (e.g., Quinton, 2020; Caligiuri, 2020) to favor 2023–2025 sources on faculty interventions, Jeffrey rejected the instruction, retaining the older studies after validating their relevance. His exact decision-making reflection regarding this literature preservation was recorded as follows:

"But I didn't follow the suggestion to remove older sources, and I kept Quinton (2020) and Caligiuri (2020) because I think they're still important."

Additionally, when seeking localized insights on social isolation within Taiwan, the system generated a broad global summary. Jeffrey rejected this output, noting that general responses required more detailed prompts to force the system to focus on Taiwanese contexts. This aligns with his defensive criteria: rejecting automated changes that erased context-specific arguments.

Perceptual Analysis (RQ2)

Prompt Design Efficacy

Thematic analysis of the qualitative interviews revealed that Jeffrey identified Context as the most critical prompt component within the PTCF framework, stating that detailed background information directly shaped clear, purpose-aligned responses. His specific outlook on the dynamic and evolving nature of prompting was explicitly stated during the interview:

"我覺得 prompt 這種事情毫無固定規則。是活的,是根據你 what you need and what you really want to change the prompt。(Translation: I think prompt is something with no fixed rules. It should be “alive”, it is based on what you need and what you really want to change the prompt.)"

Conversely, he rated Tone and Format as less impactful, noting that models often generate responses in relatively fixed academic styles regardless of minor prompt adjustments. Ultimately, Jeffrey noted that prompting features no rigid rules; rather, prompts must remain flexible and evolve based on user intent.

Contextual Bias and Localization Strategy

Jeffrey confirmed that the model's baseline suggestions leaned toward Western contexts, routinely outputting US statistical metrics when queried for institutional data. To navigate this bias, the group revised their prompts and uploaded local datasets directly, forcing the platform to ground its feedback within local contexts.

The Evaluative Balance of AI Integration

Jeffrey characterized generative AI tools as a double-edged sword. For teams with established independent viewpoints, the tool serves as a valuable resource for brainstorming and structural formatting. However, for students lacking clear authorial intent, automated feedback introduces confusion, as users cannot evaluate system suggestions against their own ideas. Jeffrey emphasized that his group established distinct ideas before using the system, allowing them to anticipate and critically filter responses rather than following suggestions blindly.

Conclusion & Expected Outcomes

The preliminary outcomes of this study support three key conclusions regarding AI-assisted writing instruction:

1. Student writers can successfully defend their voice by using context-driven criteria to accept, modify, or reject automated feedback.
2. Integrating critical composition tasks with structured prompting allows students to simultaneously develop domain-specific critical literacy and functional AI literacy.
3. While AI assists with structural text refinement, complex real-world social action designs require independent student decision-making. Future research should examine whether these interactive patterns correlate with individual learner differences or prior topic knowledge.

在人工智慧增強的批判識能寫作課程中評估人工智慧回饋

中文摘要

鑒於人工智慧(AI)的普及，以及隨之而來的批判識能教學轉變，探討學生如何在批判識能課堂中培養人工智慧識能十分重要。現有文獻強調AI回饋能夠強化識能發展，並將批判 AI與演算法權力關係視為目標。然而，將AI與批判識能結合以進行真實世界行動的研究尚有缺口。研究缺失包含學生如何採納、修改或拒絕人工智慧回饋，並評估其情境關聯性與有用性，以檢驗他們對AI能力與侷限的理解。本研究聚焦在兩個小組的其中四名學生。在課程中，學生遵循給定的批判識能框架撰寫社會行動專案，並使用結構化的提示工程來提問，以進行AI協同寫作。本研究收集了學生的寫作草稿附帶AI對話紀錄、社會行動專案、期末反思與半結構化事後訪談，並運用經濟合作暨發展組織(2025)的人工智慧識能框架以評估反思的作答與訪談對話內容，以了解學生在以社會行動為目標的前提下對提示詞的有效性與AI回饋的看法。透過觀察對 AI回饋的決策，本研究旨在檢視學生在與AI共學、共創、管理和設計過程中的思辨，為未來批判識能課堂中的AI應用提供進一步啟示。

關鍵字：人工智慧(AI)，人工智慧識能，英語作為外語寫作，批判識能，回饋，提示工程

緒論

AI回饋在現代寫作教學中展現出獨特的定位，扮演著即時、快速、個人化、客製化、具適應性、靈活且具可擴展性的教育干預角色。在標準的寫作情境中，AI回饋能有效解決表層的語言特徵，即包含文法與字詞選擇在內的結構形式要素。然而，自動化評估在處理文本銜接與連貫性等深層結構內容時，往往顯露出明顯的侷限。實證文獻顯示，學生在面對自動化修改工具時，其參與行為存在系統性的兩極化表現：個體對於涉及文法形式的回饋表現出極高的採納率，但對於涉及內容修正的回饋則伴隨著極高的拒絕率。這種評估落差在論說文與一般學術寫作任務中尤為顯著。對內容型回饋的拒絕，通常源於情境上的錯位（suggestions與學生的寫作意圖或教學預期產生衝突），或是與特定寫作主題完全不相關。至關重要的是，既有研究尚未深入調查學生在決定採納、修改或拒絕AI回饋時，究竟採取了何種具體的評估準則（研究缺口一）。

未經批判便全盤接受大型語言模型的引導，將帶來實質的教育瓶頸。麻省理工學院媒體實驗室（2025）最新的神經認知研究指出，在寫作任務中過度依賴AI工具會導致「認知負債」的累積，並對大腦的執行功能產生負面影響。雖然自動化鷹架在具備強大獨立思考的前導下能放大進階學術合成的效果，但未經反思便採納每條建議的做法會削弱學生原始的主體聲音。Pozdniakov（2026）斷言，將自動化的建議視為絕對必要的修訂，反映出內部評估判斷力與基礎AI識能的結構性匱乏。

為了建立平衡的互動機制，經濟合作暨發展組織（2025）的AI識能框架建構了分層參與歷程，融合了輸入提示（詢問）、系統能力與系統風險極限的經驗處理，以及反覆的輸出評估。成熟的AI識能要求學生在自動化工具旁展現積極參與、共創、管理與設計的能力，並靈活運用包含採納、修改或拒絕生成指令在內的明確評估操作。當寫作者配備了這些評估機制時，便能成功將AI回饋整合至以內容為核心、以主題為導向的寫作空間（例如批判識能寫作）中。傳統識能代表閱讀、寫作與表達意義的能力；而批判識能則將文本轉化為操作實踐，用以顛覆習以為常的常規、審視多元觀點、透過社會政治視角觀察議題、挑戰系統性權力階層，並落實真實世界的實踐。由於批判識能完全取決於在地化的社會、文化與政治動態，因此屬於嚴格的內容導向。正如經濟合作暨發展組織（2025）所強調，人工智慧缺乏對真實世界情境的真正掌握，這使得人類主體必須承擔獨特的情境判斷責任。Consequently, an empirical void persists regarding how student decision-making under AI feedback conditions shapes content-focused, socio-politically situated writing and subsequent real-world praxis (Gap 2)。

RQ2：學生對於批判識能寫作中提示詞設計、AI的情境對齊以及其有用性的看法幕後？

研究方法

研究背景與課程架構

本研究調查了一門為期16週、名為「批判識能：主題導向英文閱讀與寫作（一）」的必修大學部課程，研究地點位於台灣北部一所科技大學。主要研究群體包含33名Applied Foreign Languages（EFL）大一學生，其經檢驗的平均語言能力基線為CEFR B2。參與者具備基礎的功能性電腦操作能力，但在入學前均未接觸過批判識能架構。軟體部署採用OpenAI的ChatGPT GPT-5 mini平台。教學設計實踐了Lewison、Flint與van Sluys（2002）提出的批判識能三個維度架構：

1. 顛覆習以為常的常規：學生學習去解構先前被視為中立或固定不變的日常物件、信仰或政策（「事情就是這樣」）。操作：閱讀奠基性的學術文獻，並利用AI模型進行協同主題腦力激盪。
2. 審視多元觀點：識別被邊緣化的觀點、遺漏之處以及多元視角，以建構具備多層次深度的文本。操作：收集五篇外部參考文獻，並透過結構化提問促使AI挑戰單一敘事。
3. 聚焦社會政治議題：調查隱含在所選主題背後的系統性權力動態、社會結構、文化霸權與政治運作。操作：進行深層來源評估，並與AI助手共同精煉論證邏輯。
4. 採取社會行動以促進社會正義：設計出在在地化校園情境中切實可行且具備可行性的介入策略。操作：發展務實的行動設計，並利用AI引擎對執行步驟進行壓力測試。

初步結果與討論（個案分析：Jeffrey）

歷程追蹤結果 (RQ1)

PTCF提示詞建構實例

在初始的「顛覆習以為常的常規」階段，Jeffrey的小組建構了以下標準化的提示詞矩阵：

「Persona：請扮演一名小組顧問，能夠與我們討論並為我們的問題提供解答，且擅長抓取核心要點。
Tasks：請協助我識別3個在地學生、僑生及國際學生可能會抱持的常規觀念。這些觀念可能是經常被呈現為中立、或被視為『事情就是這樣』的信念，或是人們在未能批判性理解其本質與原因的情況下，便盲目接受或做出的行為與活動。
Context：我們是台灣科技大學（NTUST）大一的外語系學生。我們正在修習一門批判識能課程，要求我們應用批判識能的第一個維度，即『顛覆習以為常的常規』。我們決定的主題是：在我們的校園中，雖然充滿了在地學生、僑生以及國際學生，打他們往往更傾向於只與自己『同類』的人社交。
Examples：例如，一個『常規觀念』可能是：在地學生宏觀上可能會因為彼此之間的差異，而主動避免與國際學生溝通（以防止在某些方面冒犯到對方）。
Format：請以條列式清單呈現您的回答，並用簡單易懂的句子詳細闡述每個要點。如果可以的話，請同時提供具體範例。
Tone：請使用學術、具批判性且保持開放思維的語調。」

語言清晰度與社會政治概念的採納行為

文本推進軌跡證實，Jeffrey系統性地採納了AI針對局部句子清晰度與全局社會政治框架所提出的干預建議。在草稿四的引言中，該小組原本的表述為："From my point of view, the divide can lead to students' anxiety and pressure." 系統指出該句在結構上過於突兀且含糊，未能明確指出究竟是哪個學生群體經歷了這種隔離焦慮。隨後，模型生成了更清晰的替代文本："In my view, this divide heightens anxiety and pressure particularly for international students, who must navigate unfamiliar social norms." Jeffrey完整採納並融入了這項修改。

同樣地，在「審視多元觀點」維度中，系統建議擴展正文第一段以納入反方論點，特別是詳細說明在地學生群體如何透過無意識的特權無形中強化了社交隔離。Jeffrey接受了這項指引以深化其論證層次。此外，在進行社會政治評估時，平台指出應將學校出於善意舉辦的「新生定向說明會」視為潛在的意識形態盲點——這種活動可能從第一天起就無意中將國際群體隔離開來。Jeffrey融入了這項批判性回饋，將文本修正為：這些分開進行的特殊支援活動「可能意外地造成不公平」，並導致被邊緣化的學生「總是顯得與眾不同或被孤立」。此階段充分展示了AI如何引入複雜的學術術語來輔助學生的社會政治分析。

策略性修改與情境在地化調適

Feedback修改發生在自動化建議具有實用價值、但在實踐上必須進行在地化調整時。在設計最終的社會行動方案時，Jeffrey的小組請求AI提供舉辦開放學生論壇的策略。該語言模型建議引入商業化的金錢激勵與競爭性的獎品去吸引學生出席。雖然認同其操作上的有效性，但該小組修改了這項建議，因為商業化的競賽形式與他們預期促進平等對話的初衷存在衝突。他們最終將現金獎勵替換為免費的小組餐點，將自動化構想與團隊內部的現實限制進行了深度有機結合。

基於理性考量的拒絕動態

對於表現出過度概括或情境錯位特徵的AI回饋，Jeffrey採取了系統性的拒絕態度。當系統建議刪除較早的核心文獻引用（例如 Quinton, 2020; Caligiuri, 2020），轉而注入2023至2025年關於教職員介入的泛泛研究時，Jeffrey拒絕了這項指令，在驗證其理論關聯性後完整保留了原有的早期研究。他針對保留 these 核心文獻的具體抉擇日誌記錄如下：

"But I didn't follow the suggestion to remove older sources, and I kept Quinton (2020) and Caligiuri (2020) because I think they're still important."

此外，當小組尋求探討台灣在地社交孤立議題的見解時，系統僅生成了寬泛的全球化概述。Jeffrey果斷拒絕了該輸出，並指出這種概括性的產出需要透過更細緻的提示詞，才能強迫系統重新對齊台灣的特定情境。這完全符合其防禦性的評估標準：拒絕任何會抹去特定情境論證的自動化修正。

主題分析結果 (RQ2)

提示詞設計成效

質性訪談的主題分析顯示，Jeffrey將Context (情境) 判定為PTCF架構中最核心的提示要素。他指出，詳盡的背景資訊能直接決定產出是否具備清晰且與目標對齊的特徵。在訪談過後，他對於提示詞動態且隨使用者需求演進的特質做出了如下的精闢詮釋：

"我覺得 prompt 這種事情毫無固定規則。是活的,是根據你 what you need and what you really want to change the prompt。(Translation: I think prompt is something with no fixed rules. It should be “alive”, it is based on what you need and what you really want to change the prompt.)"

相反地，他認為 Tone (語調) 與 Format (格式) 的影響力較為微弱，因為不論提示詞做出何種微調，模型往往都會以相對固定的學術文體風格生成回應。最終，Jeffrey指出提示詞工程並不存在死板的僵化規則，提示詞本身必須保持靈活，並隨著使用者的真實意圖動態演進。

情境偏差與在地化策略

Jeffrey證實，模型的預設建議存在顯著的西方情境偏誤，在遭遇查詢請求時會慣性輸出美國的統計指標。為了克服這種偏誤，小組修正了提示詞策略並直接上傳在地數據集，以此強迫平台必須將其回饋植根於在地的脈絡中。

AI整合的評估天平

Jeffrey將生成式AI工具描繪為一把雙刃劍。對於已經建立獨立觀點的團隊而言，工具能作為腦力激盪與結構排版的無價資源；然而，對於缺乏清晰作者意圖的學生來說，自動化回饋反而會引入巨大的混淆，開展自動化調整時，使用者根本無法在沒有自身立場的參照下評估系統建議。Jeffrey強調，他的小組在與系統互動前便已確立了清晰的構想，這使他們能主動預期並批判性地篩選系統回應，而非盲目順從自動化的指令。

結論與未來展望

本研究的初步成果為AI輔助寫作教學提供了三個核心結論：

1. 學生作者能夠透過運用情境導向的評估標準來採納、修改或拒絕自動化回饋，進而成功捍衛自己的作者聲音。
2. 將具備批判性的寫作任務與結構化提示詞工程相結合，能讓學生在發展領域特定批判識能的同時，並行培養功能性的AI識能。
3. 雖然AI能有效協助結構性的文本精煉，但複雜的真實世界社會行動設計依然仰賴學生獨立的決策判斷。未來的研究應進一步檢驗這些互動模式是否與學習者的個別差異或既有先備知識存在顯著相關。

Abstract

This study is basically about how students use AI (like ChatGPT) to help them write essays. The researchers wanted to see if students just blindly accept whatever the AI says, or if they actually think critically about it—especially when they are writing about real-world social issues. They looked at how students decided to use or ignore the AI's feedback to figure out if they truly understand the limits of AI.

Introduction

We all know AI is pretty awesome at fixing grammar and spelling mistakes. But when it comes to the actual deeper meaning of an essay, AI can be a bit clueless. The problem is, if students just copy whatever the AI suggests, they stop using their own brains and lose their unique voice. This section points out that students often happily accept AI's grammar fixes but reject its ideas about the content. Why? Because the AI just doesn't understand the real-world context of what the student is trying to say.

Research Questions

The researchers were trying to answer two main things:

First: How do students decide whether to accept, change, or ignore what the AI tells them?
Second: What do students actually think about the AI's advice? Do they feel it's useful, or does it completely miss the point of their specific topic?

Literature Review

This part looks at what other scientists have already found in the past. It turns out, previous studies agree: AI is great for giving quick grammar tips, but it often gives culturally insensitive or just plain wrong advice about the bigger ideas. Because of this, students often have to reject the AI's content suggestions because it just doesn't fit what they are originally trying to write.

Methodology

To figure all this out, the researchers followed a group of 33 university students who were taking an English writing class in Taiwan. They had these students write an essay using a specific, structured way of talking to the AI so the AI wouldn't just give random answers. The researchers then collected all the essay drafts, the chat logs with the AI, and even interviewed the students to see exactly how they were making decisions about the AI's feedback.

Results & Discussion (Case: Jeffrey)

They focused closely on a few students, like one named Jeffrey. Jeffrey's case showed that he was perfectly happy letting the AI fix his sentences to make them clearer. But when the AI tried to change his main points, Jeffrey had to step in and fix the AI's ideas so they actually made sense for his topic. The main takeaway here is that students can't just let the AI do all the work; they have to actively manage and guide it so the final essay still sounds like them and makes sense in the real world.
